✦ Council Overview

The United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) is the main deliberative body of the United Nations and includes all UN Member States. It provides a forum for States to discuss international issues, cooperate on global challenges, and adopt resolutions and recommendations.

Unlike the Security Council, General Assembly resolutions are generally not legally binding. However, they can influence international cooperation and establish common positions on global issues.

The topics in this council address the relationship between States, societies, and international systems. The first focuses on language and cultural identity in post-colonial States, while the second examines how economic power can influence international relations and foreign policy.

✦ Topic A

Discussing the Normalization of Native Languages in Post-Colonial States

1. Introduction & Background

Colonial rule affected the languages used in government, education, administration, and public life in many parts of the world. In numerous post-colonial States, languages introduced or prioritized during colonial rule continued to have an important role after independence.

At the same time, many local and Indigenous languages remained widely spoken within communities but had less representation in official institutions. Governments have therefore faced questions about whether to maintain existing language systems, promote national or Indigenous languages, recognize multiple official languages, or use a combination of these approaches.

Language policy can affect education, employment, government services, cultural identity, and political participation. It can also become more complicated in countries where many languages are spoken.

International organizations have increasingly recognized the importance of protecting linguistic diversity. The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples recognizes Indigenous peoples' rights concerning their languages, while UNESCO leads the International Decade of Indigenous Languages, covering 2022–2032.

2. Key Issues & Challenges

A Education

The language used in schools can affect access to education and opportunities. Expanding education in local languages may require additional teachers, materials, and educational resources, particularly where many languages are spoken.

Governments must also consider how students can access higher education and employment when different languages are used nationally and internationally.

B Official Recognition

The recognition of languages can affect access to government services, courts, political institutions, and public information.

States with significant linguistic diversity may face practical challenges when providing services in multiple languages, while communities whose languages receive limited recognition may face difficulties participating fully in public life.

C Cultural Identity & Language Loss

Languages can transmit cultural traditions, history, and knowledge between generations. However, languages may decline when younger generations primarily use another language in education, employment, media, and government.

This creates questions about how States can preserve linguistic diversity while allowing citizens access to widely used languages.

D Economic Opportunities

Languages used in national institutions and international commerce can provide access to wider employment and educational opportunities. However, communities whose languages are less represented may face barriers in these areas.

Governments therefore face the challenge of balancing economic integration with the preservation and recognition of local languages.

E Digital Representation

The growth of digital communication has created new challenges for less widely spoken languages. Some languages have limited digital content, educational resources, and technological support.

The increasing importance of online communication has therefore created another area in which linguistic representation and preservation are being discussed.

3. International Perspectives & Stakeholders

Different States approach language policy according to their histories, populations, and political systems.

  • Post-colonial governments may seek to strengthen national languages while maintaining colonial languages that remain important in administration, education, or international communication.
  • Indigenous and linguistic communities may seek greater recognition and opportunities to use their languages in education, government, media, and cultural institutions.
  • International organizations, particularly UNESCO and relevant UN bodies, focus on linguistic diversity, cultural rights, education, and language preservation.
  • Educational institutions and civil society organizations can also contribute through language education, documentation, research, and cultural programs.

These stakeholders may share concerns about linguistic diversity while differing over the extent and methods of government involvement.

4. Previous International Action

  • The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights recognizes the right of linguistic minorities to use their own language.
  • The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, adopted by the General Assembly in 2007, includes protections concerning Indigenous languages and education.
  • The UN declared 2019 the International Year of Indigenous Languages and subsequently established the International Decade of Indigenous Languages (2022–2032), led by UNESCO.
Where things stand Despite these international initiatives, language policy remains largely determined at the national level, and States continue to differ in how local and Indigenous languages are recognized and supported.

✦ Topic B

Addressing the Use and Distribution of Economic Influence as a Tool of Foreign Policy

1. Introduction & Background

Economic relationships are an important part of international relations. States interact through trade, investment, development assistance, financial systems, energy, technology, and access to resources.

These relationships can create cooperation but can also give States different levels of influence over one another. Governments may use economic tools as part of foreign policy to encourage, discourage, or respond to the actions of another State.

Economic influence can take many forms, including sanctions, tariffs, trade agreements, foreign investment, development assistance, loans, and restrictions on specific goods or technologies.

The use of economic measures in international relations has existed for centuries. Under Article 41 of the UN Charter, the Security Council may establish measures that do not involve armed force, including economic and communications restrictions.

Globalization has increased the importance of these issues because economies are increasingly connected through international trade, financial systems, supply chains, and investment.

2. Key Issues & Challenges

A Economic Sanctions

Sanctions are a major form of economic influence. They can restrict trade, financial transactions, assets, travel, or access to particular goods.

The UN Security Council has established sanctions regimes in response to various international conflicts and security concerns. However, States differ over the effectiveness and consequences of economic sanctions, particularly when restrictions affect populations or third countries.

B Unequal Economic Power

States do not possess equal economic resources. Larger economies may have greater influence through their markets, currencies, financial systems, investment, and control over important technologies or resources.

Smaller and developing economies may be more vulnerable to economic pressure, although economic interdependence can also create costs for larger economies.

C Trade as Foreign Policy

States can use trade policies to strengthen relationships or respond to political disagreements. Tariffs, trade restrictions, and preferential agreements can therefore have both economic and diplomatic consequences.

The challenge is balancing foreign-policy objectives with the effects that trade measures can have on domestic industries, consumers, and international markets.

D Investment & Development Assistance

Economic influence can also involve positive forms of engagement, including investment, loans, infrastructure projects, and development assistance.

These relationships can support economic development and cooperation but may also create disagreements over conditions, dependence, debt, or political influence.

E Global Economic Interdependence

Modern supply chains mean that economic measures taken by one country can affect countries that are not directly involved in a dispute.

Changes to trade, investment, energy, or technology can influence prices and access to goods internationally. This makes economic policy increasingly connected to wider questions of international cooperation.

3. International Perspectives & Stakeholders

  • Major economic powers possess significant markets, financial resources, and investment capacity, giving their economic decisions international consequences.
  • Developing countries may prioritize access to trade, investment, technology, and development assistance while being concerned about economic dependence or external pressure.
  • International organizations, including the United Nations, World Trade Organization, and International Monetary Fund, play different roles in international economic relations.
  • Businesses and financial institutions can be directly affected by sanctions, trade restrictions, investment policies, and changes to international regulations.
  • Populations can experience economic measures through changes in employment, prices, access to goods, and public services.

States therefore have different perspectives depending on their economic strength, foreign-policy objectives, security interests, and relationships with other countries.

4. Previous International Action

  • The UN Charter provides a framework for Security Council economic measures, with Article 41 allowing measures that do not involve armed force.
  • The UN Security Council has established numerous sanctions regimes involving measures such as asset freezes, travel restrictions, trade restrictions, and arms embargoes.
  • The General Assembly has also addressed the use of economic measures as instruments of political and economic pressure, including concerns regarding their effects on developing countries.
  • International economic institutions such as the WTO and IMF have developed frameworks for international trade and financial cooperation.
Where things stand Disagreements continue concerning economic inequality, trade restrictions, sanctions, and the use of economic relationships for foreign-policy purposes.
